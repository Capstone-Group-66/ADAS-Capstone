1.0 Background
In 2023, traffic accidents in Canada caused 3,732 deaths and injured 118,838 people [1]. Globally, traffic accidents caused approximately 1.19 million fatalities and injured between 20 to 50 million people [2]. While various environmental factors can be attributed to some of these incidents, the majority of these accidents are caused by human-related factors. More specifically, drivers failing to perceive hazardous objects on the road, drivers failing to make correct judgments responding to hazardous objects, and drivers failing to perform maneuvers to avoid hazardous objects [3].
To compensate for these factors, automotive manufacturers have begun equipping vehicles with advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS). The purpose of ADASs is to increase vehicle and road safety by assisting drivers with driving-related tasks. ADAS accomplishes this by using sensors to collect data regarding the environment surrounding the vehicle. This data is then used by the ADAS to determine which actions the system should perform next, which can include alerting the driver of objects on the road, to taking control of the vehicle to activate the brakes. 
ADASs can come with a variety of features. These include collision warning (CW), alerting the driver of possible collisions, emergency braking assistance (EAB), automatically applying brakes to prevent collisions, lane departure warning (LDW), alerting the driver that the vehicle is leaving its lane, adaptive cruise control (ACC), automatically keeping the vehicle at a constant speed, and blind spot detection (BSD), alerting the driver of vehicle(s) in blind spots [4].
Most modern vehicles are equipped with some form of ADAS, typically including FCW and EAB. However, older vehicles typically do not come equipped with ADAS, reducing the safety of the vehicle and increasing the risk of traffic accidents [5]. This issue can be resolved through the use of aftermarket ADAS. Aftermarket ADAS are ADASs that can be installed after a vehicle has been manufactured without requiring major changes to be made to the structure of the vehicle. This allows older vehicles to gain some of the safety benefits of modern vehicles. 
Although aftermarket ADASs are a great solution to increase the safety of older vehicles, the cost of the system must also be considered. Older vehicles are typically owned by those with lower incomes [6]. Designing a low-cost aftermarket ADAS will allow older vehicles to be equipped with modern safety features, increasing road safety and decreasing traffic accidents.

2.0 Objectives
The objective of this project is to design and implement a low-cost, customizable aftermarket Advanced Driver Assistance System (ADAS) prototype capable of enhancing driver awareness and road safety.
Specifically, the system will:
1. Detect and assess hazards through AI-based lane detection, object detection, and collision risk estimation.
2. Provide real-time safety alerts to the driver via a connected mobile application with a user-friendly interface.
3. Demonstrate modularity and scalability, allowing for easy integration of additional sensors such as LiDAR.
4. Comply with legal and safety standards, ensuring that the design aligns with ethical, regulatory, and societal considerations for aftermarket automotive systems.
The outcome will be an educational and research demonstrator that showcases the feasibility of embedded AI-driven perception for low-cost vehicle safety enhancement.
2.1 Functional Requirements
2.1.1 Sensor I/O and Time:
* FR1. The front camera must intake frames >= 30 FPS with a timestamp that always increases and never repeats.
   * Frames must be taken in order of time.
   * Acceptance Criteria: 5-minute capture shows >= 30 FPS (median), with strictly increasing timestamps (100% of the time), and no out-of-order/sequence frames.
* FR2. The rear camera must intake frames >= 30 FPS with a timestamp that always increases and never repeats. Frames must be taken in order of time.
   * Rear camera required to support rear CW.
   * Acceptance Criteria: 5-minute capture shows >= 30 FPS (median), with strictly increasing timestamps (100% of the time), and no out-of-order/sequence frames.
* FR2.5. The side cameras must intake frames >= 30 FPS with a timestamp that always increases and never repeats. Frames must be taken in order of time.
   * Acceptance Criteria: 5-minute capture shows >= 30 FPS (median), with strictly increasing timestamps (100% of the time), and no out-of-order/sequence frames.
* FR3. The radar frames must be parsed with pre-detection for range, azimuth, radial velocity, and RCS. Standard radar measures:
   * Range: Measure of the distance of the object relative to the radar
   * Azimuth: Direction of the object relative to the radar (basically the angle/direction)
   * Radial Velocity: Velocity of the object relative to the radar
   * RCS: Radar cross section, how easily an object is detectable by the radar
      * Baseline: Front radar required; two rear corner radars required.
   * Acceptance Criteria: Parser outputs {range, azimuth, radial velocity, RCS} with units, and 0 parse errors over 10k frames.
* FR4. The Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) should stream with an accelerometer and gyroscope at >= 100 Hz (for smooth, detailed motion data).
   * Acceptance Criteria: IMU streams >= 100 Hz for 60 seconds with monotonic timestamps.
* FR5. The GPS data (in either NMEA or PVT) should stream at >= 5 Hz for speed and time
   * GPS gives: speed, position, and time of each reading.
   * Acceptance Criteria: GPS reports speed and position at >= 5 Hz for a 6060-second period.
* FR6. The system must have a unified timebase to sync sensor data. 
   * Additionally max inter-sensor skew should be <= 5ms over a 10-minute time period.
   * If any sensor is wireless, the intake node shall apply a receive-time timestamp and compensate for average link latency/jitter so alignment meets this requirement.
   * Acceptance Criteria: Over 10 minutes, measured inter-sensor skew <= 5ms.
* FR7. The system must be able to handle missing or delayed sensor data and continue operating with degraded functionality.
   * Acceptance Criteria: Under injected drops/errors/delays, the system should stay up and emit a degraded status
* FR8. The system will use Bluetooth as the communication network between the board and the board and the mobile app.
   * Sensors use their native wired links (MIPI/USB/CAN/Ethernet). BLE is only for phone ↔ device.
   * Acceptance Criteria: Phone to device link over BLE passes alerts and commands.
2.1.2 Sensor Calibration and Frames:
* FR11. The system must store each sensor’s settings/calibration data, and must load them at boot for the specific vehicle (make and model).
   * Store T_cam→ego and T_radar→ego (rotation + translation) in a config file (same for other sensors; sensor→ego transformation).
   * Acceptance Criteria: On boot, the system loads vehicle-specific calibration files.
* FR12. The system must define the ego frame as the vehicle-centric coordinate system.
   * All components/services must consume/emit ego coordinates. (So it should do the sensor → ego positioning transformation as pre-processing for each sensor’s service to save the main brain from having to do that). 
   * Acceptance Criteria: On boot, the system loads vehicle-specific calibration files.
* FR13. The system’s services must transform all sensor data into ego (point of view) before fusion and decision making.
   * All LDW/CW/BSD computations require using ego coordinates.
      * LDW: Lane Departure Warning (vehicle is drifting/going out of the lane)
      * CW: Collision Warning (Calculates time to collision between the ego and detected object)
         * FCW and RCW, respectively, will be implemented: Forward/Rear Collision Warnings.
      * BSD: Blind Spot Detection (calculates whether another object is inside a defined region to the left or right of the ego of the vehicle)
   * Acceptance Criteria: Each sensor service logs pre-fusion data already transformed to ego.
* FR14. The system must verify that the ego frame is lined up correctly.
   * Acceptance Criteria: Self-check aligns ego X with forward motion using GPS/IMU, handling < 5 degree errors on a straight 100m drive.

               1. M/N multi-frame confirmation
Purpose: Prevents false detections caused by noise or single-frame misclassifications.
Mechanism: A detection must appear in at least M out of the last N frames before it is used for alert data. Example:
               *                * While increasing the certainty of the alert or data passed to the main brain.
Why: Random camera or radar noise can produce one-off objects. Multi-frame logic ensures persistence before triggering warnings.


                  2. radar–camera gating by Mahalanobis distance
Purpose: Ensures fusion only combines detections that physically make sense together.
Mechanism: A detection must appear in both the radar and camera before it is used as alert data.
                  * Mahalanobis distance is a statistical distance of how far the object is apart from the camera to the radar.
                  * If this distance is small, then they are probably referring to the same thing, so fuse them.
Why: Prevents wrong associations. For example, if we accidentally fused two different cars together from camera to radar, and accidentally treated them as 1 object.


     (c) source-aware confidence fusion
Purpose: Weight detection confidence by sensor reliability before deciding to alert.
Mechanism: Each detection/alert has an associated confidence, and the final alert will only trigger if the confidence of all of the data is greater than 0.51 (51%).
Why: Different sensors will perform differently depending on the conditions, so weighing their accuracy based on that should reduce false positives when one sensor is unreliable.

2.3 Risk & Mitigation Strategies
The ADAS will be built to satisfy all provincial and federal laws; however, risks will still exist despite these considerations. The following sections will discuss some of the legal risks associated with the ADAS.
2.3.1 Sensor Installation and Calibration Risks
The process of installing sensors on a vehicle introduces several minor risks.
                              * If the sensors are not installed securely on the vehicle, they may detach while the vehicle is in motion, then it violates section 84.2 of the Highway Traffic Act and could result in a $100-$2000 fine as well as a possible license suspension for up to 60 days [10].
                              * If sensors obstruct the number plates or the front lamps of the vehicle, then it violates sections 13 and 62.7 of the Highway Traffic Act, and the driver could be guilty of an offense [10].
                              * If sensors cause the vehicle to exceed the maximum dimensions specified in section 109 of the Highway Traffic Act, then the driver could be fined $200-1000$ as well as a possible license suspension for up to 6 months [10].
If these problems occur, then some of the liability can fall on the people who installed the sensors. These risks can be mitigated by having drivers sign liability waivers waiving the installer of problems that may arise due to the installation of the sensors. These risks can also be mitigated by having precise installation guides available for each vehicle type (sedan, hatchback, minivan, etc).
Incorrectly installing and calibrating the sensors in the ADAS can result in the system not properly detecting the surroundings, resulting in the ADAS missing or producing incorrect alerts. If these errors cause the driver to get into an accident, then the installer may be partially liable for the accident. For smaller accidents, liability waivers can be used to mitigate the risks; however, if the ADAS errors cause drivers to have accidents that cause bodily harm and/or death, then the sensor installer could be charged with criminal negligence and/or homicide as specified in sections 219 and 222 of the Criminal Code, and receive prison time [14]. To mitigate these larger risks, the sensors must be thoroughly tested after they are installed and calibrated and before the ADAS is used.
2.3.2 Sensor Interference from Weather
Certain weather conditions can cause the ADAS sensors to function improperly. Conditions such as heavy rain, fog, snow, and glare can all cause the sensors to receive improper readings, causing the ADAS to create false negatives and/or false positives. This risk can be mitigated by overlapping sensors. This project will combine cameras and radars to create a sensor system that will work as intended under most environmental conditions, causing the system to produce fewer faults. This risk can also be mitigated by adding a warning and caution statement on the start-up page of the mobile application, letting users know that not all alerts and data readings are 100% accurate. This waives some of the manufacturer's responsibilities when it comes to faults and errors in the alerts. 
2.3.3 Data Privacy
The ADAS will use sensors to collect information about the environment surrounding the vehicle. This creates the risk of violating the Invasion of Privacy section in the Criminal Code. Since the Invasion of Privacy section only addresses the interference of communication and eavesdropping on communication using devices, the risks can be mitigated by completely excluding audio sensors, making the system incapable of eavesdropping or interfering with communications [14].
The system may also inadvertently collect the personal information of individuals (faces, location, address, etc). According to the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA), any personal information collected must be used in an appropriate way, and if the personal information is not used appropriately, then the organization may receive a fine (PIPEDA cite). To mitigate this risk, the ADAS will only use the data received from sensors for their intended purposes of the ADAS and not collect any of the information.
2.3.4 Distracted Driving
The ADAS uses a mobile application installed on the driver's mobile phone to send alerts to the driver. This introduces the risk of the driver being distracted by their mobile device. To mitigate this risk, the mobile application will be designed to have a hands-free mode or driver mode to satisfy section 78.3 of the Highway Traffic Act [10]. A driver mode will minimize the distractions caused by the mobile application, allowing the driver to keep their full attention on the road while also being able to receive alerts from the ADAS.
2.3.5 Cyber Security Risks
For the ADAS, the main cybersecurity risks come from the Bluetooth connections. Outside of Bluetooth, all other communication within the system is wired. If attackers gain access to the Bluetooth connections, they can potentially pass false sensor data to the system, receive data from the sensors and ADAS, or connect to the driver's mobile device. These risks can be mitigated by adding additional authentication and authorization between connections and possibly even encryption if required.

3.0 Hardware Architecture & Design
The hardware architecture of the Customizable AI-Enabled ADAS Demonstrator is centered on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kit and integrates multiple sensors, including cameras, Radar, LiDAR, GPS, and an IMU, to achieve real-time perception and decision-making. The system is modular, allowing each component to contribute distinct data that together form a unified environmental model through sensor fusion. All hardware is mounted and calibrated on a fixed platform, the 2012 Honda Civic Si, ensuring consistent testing and repeatable performance.
3.1 NVIDIA Jetson Board 
The NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kit serves as the central processing unit for the customizable AI-Enabled ADAS Demonstrator. This embedded computing platform is purpose-built for AI and robotic applications, offering high-performance GPU acceleration, energy efficiency, and versatile I/O connectivity. These capabilities make it ideal for real-time perception, sensor fusion, and control tasks in automotive environments [16], [17]. The board is equipped with an Ampere GPU featuring 1,024 CUDA cores and 32 Tensor Cores, delivering up to 67 trillion operations per second (TOPS), which enables accelerated deep learning inference and computer vision processing [16]. Its 6-core ARM Cortex-A78AE CPU operating at 1.7 GHz, combined with 8 GB of LPDDR5 memory, supports high-speed processing of multi-sensor data streams for real-time decision-making [16]. Operating at a low power envelope of up to 25 Watts, the board maintains manageable thermal performance while powering all connected sensors and cameras [16].
In terms of connectivity, the Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kit provides a wide array of interfaces, including GPIO, I²C, SPI, UART, USB 3.1, M.2 NVMe, and PCIe, allowing flexible integration with radar, LiDAR, cameras, IMUs, and other peripheral devices [17]. It is fully compatible with the NVIDIA JetPack SDK, which offers a comprehensive suite of libraries and tools for AI, computer vision, deep learning, and GPU acceleration [17]. The board functions as the central hub for sensor data acquisition, processing, and fusion into real-time ego frames. It collects inputs from all onboard/connected sensors and performs the necessary calculations before fusion [17]. Its GPU and CPU enable simultaneous execution of multiple AI inference pipelines, including object detection, motion estimation, and collision prediction [16]. It is able to process multiple sensor signals, both digital and analog, directly through GPIO and dedicated serial/I²C ports to ensure synchronized data acquisition [17]. The modular design of the board supports future upgrades, such as higher-resolution cameras, additional AI workloads, or advanced radar units [18].
Calibration and system alignment are handled natively on the Jetson Orin Nano Super. Calibration routines ensure proper alignment between sensors and the vehicle’s ego frame (T_sensor → ego), which is critical for accurate spatial modeling [17]. Sensor fusion algorithms also run directly on the platform, enabling low-latency decision-making and high-accuracy environmental modeling [18]. 3.2 Sensor Integration
This Customizable AI-Enabled ADAS Demonstrator integrates a diverse array of sensors that work in tandem to construct a comprehensive, real-time perception of the vehicle’s surrounding environment. Each sensor, ranging from cameras and radar to LiDAR, GPS, and IMU, contributes data that captures visual, spatial, and motion-related information. These sensors are strategically positioned around the vehicle to maximize coverage and minimize blind spots. By leveraging sensor fusion techniques, the system combines these inputs into a unified situational awareness model, enabling accurate object detection, lane boundary recognition, and precise vehicle localization. This integrated perception layer forms the foundation for downstream decision-making modules, such as lane departure warnings (LDW), forward collision warnings (FCW), and blind spot detection (BSD), ensuring timely and context-aware alerts to the driver. It supports high-frequency streaming at ≥100 Hz, satisfying FR3 requirements for smooth motion tracking. Orientation outputs include rotation vector, linear acceleration, gravity vector, and raw gyroscope and accelerometer data. These outputs are essential for ego-motion tracking and fallback velocity estimation (FR52), and they contribute to the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) fusion process for object tracking and collision prediction (FR50) [22].
Sensor data from the BNO085 is timestamped and aligned to the system’s unified timebase (FR5), then transformed into the ego frame for consistent spatial modeling (FR13). The module is plug-and-play with Jetson Nano using either I²C or UART, and its compact form factor and low power consumption make it well-suited for embedded automotive systems. Calibration routines will be implemented to ensure consistent alignment across vehicle setups (FR15). The sensor’s built-in classification features, such as step detection and activity recognition, may also be leveraged for future ADAS feature expansion [22].
3.2.5 LiDAR (Optional): SLAMTEC RPLIDAR C1 360° DTOF Laser Scanner


The SLAMTEC RPLIDAR C1 360° DTOF Laser Scanner is included as an optional component in the ADAS demonstrator, primarily supporting Blind Spot Detection (BSD). Its fusion-type Direct Time of Flight (DTOF) technology enables high-definition distance and reflectivity measurements with minimal blind zones, making it ideal for short-range lateral monitoring around the vehicle [23].
The RPLIDAR C1 offers a maximum detection radius of 12 meters, with enhanced performance in close-range scenarios. It provides 2.5D reflectivity data alongside positional information, improving object classification and lateral zone awareness. The sensor’s 360° horizontal field of view and SL-DTOF ranging system allow for continuous scanning of adjacent lanes and blind spot regions. Classified as an eye-safe Class 1 laser product, it is suitable for continuous operation in passenger environments. Its compact form factor and anti-interference design make it well-suited for embedded automotive systems [23].
To maximize coverage of lateral zones, the LiDAR will be mounted on the center roof of the vehicle, providing unobstructed 360° visibility. Scan frames will be timestamped and aligned to the system’s unified timebase (FR5), then transformed into the ego frame (FR13). Data will be fused with radar and side camera inputs to enhance BSD alert precision and reduce missed detections (FR32–FR33). The sensor’s short-range precision and panoramic coverage make it a valuable enhancement to the ADAS blind spot monitoring system.

4.0 Methods: Architecture, Algorithms, and DevOps
The methods section will serve as our proposal for modeling the software side of system behaviour. This section outlines key architectural decisions, software components, communication frameworks, and design patterns employed to ensure real-time performance, fault tolerance, and interoperability.  Leveraging the Jetson Nano’s capabilities, the architectural goal is to support all of the software systems' functional requirements.
4.1 Pipeline Architecture for Preprocessing & Alert Detection [24]
4.1.1 Justification (best-fit)
The desirable architectural characteristics/goals of the Main Brain are:
                                    * Low latency & consistent throughput (FR1–7, FR40–41, FR50, FR60–63): must maintain a strict 20 Hz tick rate while in-taking multiple sensors and running four camera models in parallel.
                                    * Determinism & replayability (FR80–81, FR90–96): identical inputs → identical outputs; bounded skew; warm-up gates; deterministic drop rules.
                                    * Reliability under degradation (FR6, FR22, FR34, FR51): tolerate missing/late frames, suppress when quality is low, and keep tracking life-cycle.
                                    * Modularity & swappability (D-001/D-007/D-009; FR18): fixed mount identities with swappable devices; intrinsics/extrinsics and zones in YAML; optional LiDAR plug-in.
                                    * Testability (FR111–112): stage boundaries with stable contracts; easy fault injection (late/out-of-order frames) and deterministic assertions.


A staged pipeline is the best fit architecture style for Preprocessing & Alert detection since each sensor/component runs in its own thread and writes to a small single-producer/single-consumer queue, while one 20 Hz “tick” thread reads the latest sample from each source, lines them up by when we received them, fuses them, and makes the alerts. This keeps latency low and prevents a slow camera from blocking everything else. It also gives repeatable timing targets (≤ 5 ms skew, ~150 ms buffer, ~100 ms late-drop, ~2 s warm-up). All heavy GPU work (TensorRT per camera) stays outside the tick, so the tick stays steady. Most behavior is in config, not code (zones, χ² gate 5.99, M/N = 3/5, sensor weights like radar 1.0, camera 0.6, lidar2d 0.25), so we can tune safely. LiDAR is optional by design—if it’s there, we only use it to boost BSD confidence below 20 kph and never as a sole trigger. Therefore, the choice of a Pipeline Architecture best hits our goals: low latency, deterministic output we can replay, graceful handling of missing/late data, and clean swaps of sensors later.
4.1.2 Alternatives rejected
                                    * Single-loop monolith (one big thread): This option was rejected since camera capture, model inference, radar parsing, and EKF in one loop causes long worst-case latencies and missed deadlines under load. GPU work blocks everything. Determinism suffers when I/O stalls.
                                    * ROS-style fine-grained graph (many nodes): This option was rejected because of the extra scheduling and serialization overhead on a Jetson-class board; time sync and replay determinism are harder across many processes. It would also introduce added jitter and failure modes without a clear benefit for our fixed, on-device ideology (FR90-96).
4.2 Interfaces & Data Contracts
4.2.1 Frame/Packet Header (Authoritative)
Each frame/pack will be given this authoritative header (C++ snippet of locked Header struct):
struct Header {
  uint64_t t_device_ns;  // device clock if provided
  uint64_t t_ingest_ns;  // authoritative monotonic receive time
  Mount    mount;  // fixed mount id (see enum)
  uint32_t seq;  // per-mount sequence counter
  bool     healthy;  // device/ingest health bit
};


Why: We align and fuse on ingest timestamps to meet skew budgets even with potential wireless links; device time is retained for diagnostics/testing.
                                    * Ingest-time as truth: Wireless jitter makes device time unreliable; ingest-time guarantees ≤ 5 ms skew at the 20 Hz tick
                                    * seq & healthy: Needed for loss/dup detection, health telemetry, and deterministic replay assertions.

4.2.4 Tracking and Fusion Outputs

struct Track {

  Eigen::Matrix4f P; // 4x4 covariance

};
Justification/usage
                                    * State choice [x,y,vx,vy]: Constant-velocity EKF state is the simplest stable model for TTC and BSD occupancy; it avoids over-fitting acceleration noise.
                                    * P covariance: Required for Mahalanobis gating and for downstream confidence math; kept PSD for numerical stability.
                                    * Id persistence: Satisfies FR35 and lets alerts reference the causal object (object_id).
                                    * sources & conf_01: Make the fusion output self-describing—debuggable source and a single scalar for decision thresholds/FR7

4.3.1.1 On‑Site/On-Vehicle Measurement Process (to fill xyz_m & rpy_deg)
Ego frame: origin O at midpoint of rear wheel contact patches on level ground; +X forward, +Y left, +Z up.
For each sensor:
                                    1. Reference point: lens center (cams), antenna face center (radars), board center (IMU), spin axis (LiDAR).
                                    2. Ground projection: plumb bob to mark point P on the floor beneath the reference.
                                    3. Positions (m):
                                    * x = signed distance from O to P along centerline (+ forward)
                                    * y = signed perpendicular distance from centerline to P (+ left)
                                    * z = vertical distance floor→reference (laser level)
                                    4. Orientations (deg) using a digital angle gauge on a flat plate aligned to the sensor's forward axis:
                                    * yaw (about Z) vs. centerline (left = +)
                                    * pitch (about Y) vs. level (down = −)
                                    * roll (about X) vs. level (left side up = +)
                                    5. Calibrate & refine:
                                    * Cameras: intrinsics + extrinsics (Charuco/AprilTag); target reprojection error <0.5 px.
                                    * Radars: dynamic boresight yaw via straight‑road runs & overtakes; aim +/-1.5°.
                                    * Update YAML with calibrated values; keep photos and a short report.

4.4 Threads & Queues
Why this structure: We read many sensors at once and still need a steady 20 Hz output. Each sensor (or model) runs in its own thread and passes data through lock‑free SPSC queues. Queues are pre‑sized, so memory is predictable and drops are visible. A single 20 Hz tick thread then picks the latest sample ≤ t_tick, enforcing ≤5 ms skew, 150 ms buffer, and 100 ms late‑drop. Optional sources (only the LiDAR2D) simply don’t start a thread, so the tick logic doesn’t change. The result: parallel capture/inference, with deterministic alignment, fusion, and decisions.

4.5 Stages (Expanded) + Parallelism
4.5.1 Stage A — Ingest & Timestamp (Parallel)
Responsibility: Get raw samples from each physical device, stamp them with a timestamp (authoritative), and feed it into the correct per-sensor queue. This stage is where we know the timing and health per sensor. All threads will capture/poll from device SDKs and dump outputs + timestamp into the correct queue.


Steps:
                                    1. Decode/grab raw data: Then stamp it with a Header (recall {t_ingest_ns, mount, seq, healthy})
                                    2. For Cameras: Fix resolution from intrinsics measured in the respective YAML file; push CameraFrame to the queue.
                                    3. For Radars: Parse points or targets; IMU at ~100Hz; And LiDAR is active.


Produces: CameraFrame, RadarTargets, ImuSample, LidarDetections (each with Header).
4.5.2 Stage B — Camera Preproc + Inference (Parallel: 4 Threads/4 CUDA Streams)
Responsibility: This stage turns the raw camera frames into clean, normalized detections (detections are found with the ONNX model file previously trained + OpenCV) that are ready for fusion. Each camera and thread runs independently, so a slower camera won’t stall others. This stage takes Q.Cam.*.Frame (all camera frames from the queue) and outputs all detections into Q.Det.*.


Steps:
                                    1. Load undistort maps once, and cv::remap each frame.
                                    2. Resize/letterbox + normalize (manifest mean/std) → TensorRT enqueueV2(stream). If TRT fails, OpenCV DNN is the fallback.
                                    3. OpenCV NMS → DetBatch{Header, dets}


Produces: DetBatch per camera at camera FPS.
4.5.3 Stage C — Radar Processing (Parallel: 3 Threads)
Responsibility: This stage converts the vendor radar frames into the stable structure we defined RadarTarget + RadarTargets (so we don’t rely on any proprietary vendor hardware+software combo). Each object carries a range and Doppler for gating/TTC. Measurements should be kept in the radar frame until fusion. Input = vendor frames (radar).


Steps:
                                    1. Parse → cluster to object‑like targets with {range, az, radial_vel, rcs, σ*}. Kept in the radar frame until fusion.


Produces: RadarTargets for Front, RearCornerL, and RearCornerR.
4.5.4 Stage D — LiDAR Processing (Optional, Parallel)
Responsibility: This stage is skipped/disabled if LiDAR is not present. If present, extract near-field clusters for BSD confidence at low speeds (<20kph). Never acts as the sole trigger for an alert. Turn off/disable when V_ego > 20kph. Input = LiDAR scans.


Steps:
                                    1. Filter ground/noise.
                                    2. Cluster near-fields/points.
                                    3. Producer pauses when V_ego > 20kph.
                                    4. Convert to detections. Note: LiDAR is never the sole trigger (only boosts BSD confidence).


Produces: LidarDetections gated by speed policy.
4.5.5 Stage E — 20 Hz Alignment & Fusion/Tracking (Single Thread for Determinism)
Responsibility: This stage is the deterministic single thread that aligns all sensor data to the current tick. It also associates all measurements, tracks Track objects using EKF, computes the lane model, estimates ego speed, and emits a consistent snapshot. Inputs = taken at each tick from queues.


Steps:
                                    1. Transform ALL to ego: radar polar {r,az}→(x,y); camera dets → bearing corridors via camera extrinsics; LiDAR {r,θ}→(x,y).
                                    2. Associate & gate: Mahalanobis gate χ²(2) =< 5.99 (FR34b) between radar targets and camera hypotheses; LiDAR (if present) gates to nearby tracks with low weight.
                                    3. M/N confirmation: require M=3 out of N=5 before promoting to a live track.
                                    4. EKF update: Update state [x,y,vx,vy] with constant‑velocity model. And source‑aware confidence per fusion_params.source_weights.
                                    5. Lane Model v1: FrontCam → undistort → BEV → thresholds/edges → RANSAC poly fit → {lane_width, curvature, quality} → gives lane model.
                                    6. Ego speed: IMU+GPS.
                                    7. FINAL: Outputs FusedFrame{t_tick_ns, tracks[], lane} → Q.Tick.Fused.


Produces: FusedFrame once per tick (20 Hz) with tracks + lane model.
4.5.6 Stage F — Decision Logic (Inside Tick Thread, After Fusion, Stage E)
Responsibility: This stage converts the fused states from Stage E into the alerts that will be sent to the user. It takes as inputs = FusedFrame, zones, fusion_params, V_ego.


Steps (alerts to detect):
                                    1. CW-Front/Rear: select in‑path tracks (zones.in_path + lane); TTC = dist/(V_ego−V_obj); if not closing → ∞; trigger when TTC < threshold and holds for N/M; set direction
                                    2. LDW: use D_Ego‑Lane; activation>clearing; N/M; 2 s cooldown; suppress on “Lane Unavailable”
                                    3. BSD: test BSD polygons; 1s cooldown; suppress when V_ego < 1 m/s for 5 s; LiDAR (if present & <20 kph) may raise confidence only.
                                    4. Each alert includes all FR70 schema fields.


Produces: std::vector<Alert> per tick, ready for the main brain/app.
4.5.7 Stage G — Publish / Main‑brain handoff
Responsibility: Deliver tick outputs to consumers without blocking the tick. Publish FusedFrame and Alerts on the respective alerts queues/topics. 
                                    * A small publisher drains Q.Tick.Fused and Q.Tick.Alerts and exposes topics /fused and /alerts for the main brain (Hexagonal main brain) to consume per tick.

4.8 Main Brain Feature Set & Rationale
4.8.1 Scope and Role
The Main Brain part of the software will run on the Jetson board. It consumes the fused perceptions outputs and alerts decisions from the pipeline previously discussed. It also ensures freshness, lifecycle rules, and publishes alerts and status to the phone over BLE.


Note: This part of the software does NOT render maps, run heavy ML, or compute phone GPS (these functionalities are delegated to the pipeline and mobile application)
This is to best align with:
                                    * FR50-63 having the decision/fusion already made ahead in the pipeline
                                    * FR70-76 publishing method and constraints
                                    * FR71/78 regarding BLE pairing (and potential Wi-Fi Direct pairing)


4.8.2 Inputs, Contracts, and Constraints
Queues (authoritative):
                                    * Q.Tick.Alerts: std::vector<Alert> at 20 Hz. Read-only, SPSC.
                                    * Q.Tick.Fused: FusedFrame at 20 Hz. Read-only, SPSC.
Alert schema (FR70):
                                    * Preserve the semantics and fields from the alert when sending it over BLE
Timebase:
                                    * 20 Hz; <= 5ms skew, warm-up period
Phone UX/GPS (FR72–73, FR74):
                                    * All indicators are on the phone.
                                    * Maps and GPS live solely on the phone.


4.8.3 Feature Set & Justification
This section outlines the required feature set for the Main Brain part of the software architecture. Features/functionalities needed were derived from functional requirements, project description and the architecture of the pipeline and mobile application.
4.8.3.1 F.01 Tick Intake and Freshness
                                    * Behavior: Pull all alerts and fused frames at 20 Hz from queues. Zero-copy reads.
                                    * TTL rule: Drop any alert where now_ms - alert.t_ms > alert.ttl_ms.
                                    * Why: FR70 requires time-bounded messages; TTL at the consumer is the highest leverage single point of truth. It prevents stale bursts after reconnect and keeps replays deterministic.
4.8.3.2 F.02 Resend Cache (On Reconnect)
                                    * Behavior: A ring buffer holds the latest 40 ticks of alerts (around 2 seconds). The ring buffer should replay non-expired alerts on reconnect. 
                                    * TTL rule: TTL rule still applies; Drop any alert where now_ms;  alert.t_ms > alert.ttl_ms.
                                    * Why: Preserves the user’s context in the case of transient drops without violating TTL.
4.8.3.3 F.03 BLE Transport (FR71/78)
                                    * GATT Services: ADAS Core with four characteristics (Characteristic = named data endpoint inside a BLE service; holds bytes, properties, and metadata):
                                    * ADAS_AlertStream (Notify): one-way, low-latency alert delivery fits Notify; 20 Hz push (if Q.Tick.Alerts is not empty).
                                    * ADAS_Status (Notify/Read): 1 Hz push; pull for on-demand.
                                    * ADAS_Command (Write): infrequent app→device control.
                                    * ADAS_Pair (Write/Notify): setup/pairing endpoint.
                                    * Phone connects over BLE
                                    * App writes the 4-digit code to the ADAS_Pair characteristic
                                    * Then the device compares it with the code on board and notifies with the result:
                                    * Match → “ok” store this phone as an allowed device.
                                    * No match → “deny”
                                    * MTU negotiation: On connect, ATT_MTU is negotiated by the stack. Code queries the granted MTU and fragments payloads accordingly.
                                    * Example: say MTU = 100 bytes. Then the usable payload_size = MTU - 7 bytes
                                    * So for a 250-byte alert, it would be sent as 93 bytes, 93 bytes, 64 bytes.
                                    * Encoding: CBOR on the wire, JSON in logs/dev tools.
                                    * Why CBOR on wire?
                                    * CBOR (Concise Binary Object Representation) is a compact form of JSON (RFC 8949 [29])
                                    * Shown to cut bytes by 30-50% compared to JSON.
                                    * Retain the JSON on each end for human readability (debugging).
4.8.3.4 F.04 Status Publisher (FR75–76)
                                    * Payload (sent every 1 Hz): { t_ms, link_state, cam_fps, model_fps, latency_ms_p50_p95, temp_C, disk_free_GB, mode: live|replay, ttl_drops_total }
                                    * Why: First hardware proof and fields for future debugging. Low frequency to avoid collisions with more important alerts.
4.8.3.5 F.05 Alert Output Logging for Latency (FR80–81/83)
                                    * Record at publish: { t_ms_out, alert_ids[], mtu_used, fragments_emitted, retries }
                                    * Why: The pipeline already takes care of replaying upstream queues. This output log exists only to measure link latency, serialization, and fragmentation.
4.8.3.6 F.06 Minimal Command Set (App → Board)
                                    * Essentials: Ping, Ack
                                    * Admin/Dev: ForgetAllPairings, LoadReplay, StartReplay, ExitReplay.
                                    * Dropped: User MuteOn/Off and RequestLogs.
                                    * Why drop mute: App can mute its own audio/visuals (FR72). Avoids split-brain states.
                                    * Why drop log pull: In practice, logs will be pulled from the board directly (since not a commercial product).
4.8.3.7 F.07 Replay Mode (Minimal)
                                    * Scope: Publish alerts from an alert log to the phone to validate the BLE link and app UX without running the pipeline. Should be disabled if replay is playing on Pipeline. Only for replaying or testing interactions between the Main Brain & the Mobile App.
                                    * Why: Pipeline replay covers algorithmic correctness; this covers transport/UI. Keeps live vs replay explicit via mode to avoid confusion (FR75).
4.8.3.8 F.08 Security Check (FR78)
                                    * Pairing: Done with 4-digit console code. Link once, save device. Require basic encryption after linking. 
                                    * ForgetAllPairings: if the command is received, all saved devices in the list are purged.
                                    * Why: Pipeline replay covers algorithmic correctness; this covers transport/UI. Keeps live vs replay explicit via mode to avoid confusion (FR75).
4.8.3.9 F.09 Failure Handling
                                    * BLE disconnects: Keep consuming and logging alerts; hold 40 ticks of alerts in the ring buffer; exponential backoff reconnect attempts with (starts at 200ms, capped at 5 seconds)
                                    * Reconnect backlog handling: Send non-expired alerts; drop expired first, and increment respective counters.
                                    * MTU changes mid-session: Update MTU and repack next tick (to fit the new Maximum transmission unit size).
                                    * Invalid command/schema: NACK via Ack with code; log and ignore.
                                    * Send an acknowledgement via Ack with ok: false and error detail or code.
                                    * Thermal: Raise status.thermal=warning at vendor threshold; if critical, throttle status to 0.2 Hz and shut off alert transmission and preserve alerts.
                                    * Disk space low: Flag in status heartbeat; display on mobile UI.
                                    * Serialization failure: Retry once; then drop that alert tick if it fails again. Increment serialize_fail_total.
                                    * Ring Buffer Timeliness Rule: Time-bounded ring buffer that pops the oldest ticks without blocking; count evictions by incrementing the respective counter.
                                    * Watchdog: Pet (send a watchdog keep alive tick) on successful publish or idle loop; on SIGTERM, stop readers, flush, unregister GATT, exit (FR91–96).
4.8.4 Metrics for QA of Feature Set (FR-Acceptance)
This section outlines the counters, gauges (dynamic env variables), and timers that the feature set will require.


Counters:
                                    * alerts_sent_total: counts # of alerts serialized and accepted by BLE
                                    * fragments_emitted_total: counts # of fragments emitted after MTU packing
                                    * fragments_retried_total: counts # of fragments queued for retransmission.
                                    * reconnects_total: count # of GATT reconnect events
                                    * ttl_drops_total: count # of alerts dropped by TTL filter
                                    * status_publishes_total: count # of status notifications sent


Gauges:
                                    * mtu_active_bytes: the current negotiated value of the MTU of maximum bytes on the link.
                                    * ring_depth_alerts: counts how full the ring buffer is.


Timer:
                                    * latency_ms: latency measure from dequeue → final fragment submitted; track p50/p95.
                                    * Example: Use for acceptance guarding p95 (latency_ms < 40 ms)


4.9 BLE Alert Framing & Fragmentation
The purpose of this section is to outline how the Main Brain packages and sends alerts over BLE so the phone gets complete, ordered data with low delay. Here, we define the frame header, CBOR payload, fragmentation rules, and send and receive steps to keep the link reliable.
4.9.1 Definitions
                                    * Tick: 20 Hz decision ticks (every 50 ms)
                                    * Alert batch: All alerts from one tick (50 ms period) – ticks 0..k
                                    * Tick payload: CBOR payload that wraps one tick’s alerts
                                    * Frame: One BLE Notify value = Header (4B) + Slice of payload (rest of Bytes)
                                    * Usable bytes: usable = ATT_MTU - 7 (since 3 B ATT/GATT + 4 B header)
4.9.2 Frame Header (4 bytes)
Frame header structure locked in C++:
struct Header {
  uint16  tick_id;   // wraps mod 65536 – wraps after around an hour
  uint8   seq_no;    // 0..seq_max
  uint8  seq_max;   // 0-based max; fragments per tick = seq_max+1
}
                                    * tick_id: floor(t_ms / 50ms) mod 65536. Monotonic per boot.’
                                    * seq_no: order/sequence of the tick starting from 0; gives the order packets that need to be reassembled in.
                                    * seq_max: 0-based index of the last fragment for the tick. Total fragments = seq_max + 1.


Note: Rename struct to BLEHeader when creating to avoid collision with the Header struct previously defined. The header in this section will refer to the BLEHeader in practice.
4.9.3 Tick Payload (variable length)
Tick payload structure locked in C++ (logical schema before CBOR encoding):
struct TickPayload {
  uint16_t tick_id;  // same meaning as header
  uint8_t  seq_max;  // 0-based max; fragments per tick = seq_max+1
  uint8_t  n;  // number of alerts in this tick (0..k)
  std::vector<Alert> alerts; // length == n; FR70 alert objects
};
                                    * tick_id: floor(t_ms / 50ms) mod 65536. matches Header.tick_id
                                    * seq_max: matches Header.seq_max; included for integrity checks.
                                    * n: count of alerts bundled into this tick payload
                                    * alerts[]: the n alerts bundled for this tick


A Slice is a chunk of the CBOR TickPayload. Slices are never sent without an attached Header. Many Slices can be sent under the same header as long as CBOR(TickPayload) < slice_cap in bytes, and the payload isn’t full (send under 1 single frame).


4.9.4 Coalescing policy
The goal of this policy is to minimize the number of frames per tick while delivering all relevant alerts.
                                    * Default: Put all non-expired alerts of the tick into ONE tick payload with a header and send through the BLE link.
                                    * Encode all alerts together as one TickPayload
                                    * Send it. If the payload is bigger than slice_cap, it will be split into multiple frames automatically.
                                    * Oversized case (extremely rare): If any single alert is oversized, send only that alert this tick. Condition to check if oversized alert: len(alert_CBOR) > slice_cap.
                                    * All other alerts from the same tick are deferred by one tick (50 ms).
                                    * The oversized alert will still be split across multiple frames; the header gives seq_no/seq_max for reassembly.
                                    * Send one oversized alert per tick, largest first.
                                    * Empty tick: If there are no alerts, send nothing.
                                    * TTL first: Drop expired alerts before coalescing.
                                    * On MTU change: Recompute slice_cap on the next tick only


4.9.5 Sending Process (each tick @ 20 Hz)
                                    1. Drain queues and Filter
                                    1. Read these tick alerts.
                                    2. Drop any alerts where now_ms - t_ms > ttl_ms
                                    3. If none, send nothing
                                    2. Compute sizes
                                    1. Read current ATT_MTU from stack → slice_cap = ATT_MTU - 7 (accounting for header).
                                    2. CBOR-encode each alert to know len_i in bytes.
                                    3. Choose payload (coalescing step)
                                    1. If any len_i > slice_cap → oversized case: payload = that single alert (pick the largest if several). Defer other alerts one tick.
                                    2. Else → payload = ALL remaining alerts
                                    4. Encode tick
                                    1. Build TickPayload{tick_id, n, alerts[...]}.
                                    2. CBOR-encode to contiguous buffer B.
                                    5. Fragment
                                    1. frag_count = ceil(len(B) / slice_cap).
                                    2. For i = 0 .. frag_count-1:
                                    1. Header = {tick_id, seq_no=i, seq_max=frag_count-1}.
                                    2. Slice = B[i*slice_cap : min((i+1)*slice_cap, len(B))]
                                    3. gatt_notify(ADAS_AlertStream, Header||Slice).
                                    6. After sending
                                    1. Record metrics (alerts sent, fragments, latency).
                                    2. Store the exact CBOR tick payload B plus tick_id in a small ring for reconnect, resend.
                                    7. If BLE disconnects mid-tick
                                    1. Stop sending immediately. Do not retry fragments.
                                    2. Keep payload B in the ring for resend after reconnect with respect to TTL expiry.


Each fragment is sent as Header{tick_id, seq_no, seq_max} || Slice_i. The header repeats on every fragment.
4.10 Hexagonal architecture for Main Brain [30]
4.10.1 Justification (best-fit)
The desirable architectural characteristics/goals of the Main Brain are:
                                    * Low latency (FR70-76)
                                    * Determinism (FR80-81/83-96)
                                    * Reliability (FR70/75–76/91–96)
                                    * Modifiability (FR71/78)
                                    * Testability (FR80–81)
The Hexagonal architecture best meets these goals by using a Reactor that owns BLE I/O to cut queuing and send frames immediately; a single tick payload per 50 ms with fixed rules for TTL, coalescing, and fragmentation (slice_cap = ATT_MTU - 7) so identical inputs yield identical frames; a resend ring and explicit reconnect budget so brief drops recover within ttl_ms, plus watchdog pet on progress for safe restarts; ports that isolate transport, storage, time, and pairing so Wi-Fi Direct or different BLE stacks swap in without touching domain logic; and pure-core logic behind small interfaces so we can unit-test TTL, packing, and fragmentation with fake TickSource/Time/Transport, achieving hardware-free, deterministic tests.
4.10.2 Alternatives rejected
                                    * Layered Architecture: Transport callbacks bleed into the domain. Harder to swap BLE to Wi-Fi. Determinism suffers under I/O contention. Clearly less applicable to desired ACs.
                                    * Microservices Architecture: Extra schedulers and IPC add jitter and failure modes on a single board. No upside with just two external interfaces (queues + BLE). So again, it is clearly less applicable to the desired ACs for the main brain.


4.10.3 Style overview and adaptation to context
Hexagonal = “ports and adapters” around a small core. Reactor = event loop for handling I/O. We are deciding to implement the core as a single class to match team size and the expected scope of the Main Brain. In researching this pattern, often the “core” is split into different smaller parts. Given that the expected scope of the Main Brain is smaller than typical projects using this pattern, we’ve opted for a leaner approach reducing code complexity, but making maintainability a bit more difficult (since it’s a small section, if a rework is required, it may take a tiny bit longer to refactor with the change, but not a significant amount of extra time). 
                                    * Core (one class): Implements TTL filter, coalescing, CBOR encoding, fragmentation, resend, and metrics. No BLE APIs, no files, no system clocks inside. All external effects go through ports.
                                    * Ports (interfaces): Inbound (ticks, time, commands). Outbound (transport, status, logs, pair store, watchdog). Keep them tiny. They define what the core needs, not how it’s done (interfaces)
                                    * Adapters: Implementations of: BLE GATT transport, SPSC queue reader, filesystem logger, pairing store, Linux watchdog.
                                    * Reactor: A small event loop that owns the transport and timers. It reads app commands, handles connects/MTU changes, and drains a send queue.
Data flow overview:
Q.Tick.Alerts → TTL filter → coalesce per tick → CBOR encode → fragment into frames Header{tick_id,seq_no,seq_max} || Slice → ADAS_AlertStream (Notify).
                                    * In parallel: 1 Hz Status to ADAS_Status. Ring buffer caches recent CBOR TickPayloads for reconnect replay within TTL.
4.10.4 Ports and adapters (why each is required)
Inbound ports:
                                    * ITickSource: Reads Q.Tick.Alerts at 20 Hz. Isolates SPSC specifics (behind interfaces) and makes tick intake mockable. Ties to FR50–63 context.
                                    * ITime: now_ms(), tick_id(). Single on-board clock for TTL and headers. Needed for FR70 freshness and deterministic replay
                                    * ICommands: delivers parsed commands (Ping/Ack/admin). Keeps BLE parsing outside the core. Aligns with FR71/78.
Outbound ports:
                                    * ITransport: att_mtu_bytes() and sendFrame(tid,seq,seq_max,slice). Uses BLE today; Wi-Fi later is a drop-in adapter. Critical for FR71 link behavior.
                                    * IStatusSink: Publishes 1 Hz statuses. Keeps debug HUD details out of the core.
                                    * ILogSink: writes outputted records {t_ms_out, alert_ids[], mtu_used, fragments_emitted, retries} for latency/frag analysis
                                    * IPairStore: allow/forget device tokens. Satisfies FR78 without coupling to BLE storage.
                                    * IWatchdog: pet/disable watchdog temporarily. Implements FR91–96 clean restart behavior safely
Adapters:
                                    * BLEGattTransport (GATT Notify/Write, MTU callbacks, reconnect).
                                    * SPSCQueueTickSource (lock-free reads from Q.Tick.*)
                                    * FsLogger (binary output packet log).
                                    * PairStorage (store on-disk an allowlist of devices)
                                    * LinuxWatchdog (utilize chosen Linux distros watchdog and keep it alive/pet after each outputted record)
4.10.5 Core components (inside the single core class)
The class groups single responsibility, single purpose methods. They are methods, not separate modules or classes.
4.10.5.1 Methods/Functions required:
                                    * ttlFilter(..): drops now_ms − t_ms > ttl_ms. Single point of freshness (FR70).
                                    * coalesce(...): one TickPayload per tick. If any len(alert_CBOR) > slice_cap, send that alert alone; defer others by one tick. Otherwise, bundle all non-expired alerts (same logic as described in the coalesce policy).
                                    * toCbor(..): FR70 schema converted to → CBOR bytes (on-wire); JSON only for logs/tools/human viewing.
                                    * fragmentAndSend(...): slice_cap = att_mtu_bytes() − 7; frag_count = ceil(len(B)/slice_cap); for each i: send Header{tick_id,i,frag_count−1} || Slice_i.
                                    * cache(...): store {tick_id, CBOR TickPayload, t_ms} in a small ring (40 ticks long) for reconnect replay; purge by TTL rule.
                                    * buildStatus(...): { t_ms, link_state, cam_fps, model_fps, p50/p95_latency_ms, temp_C, disk_free_GB, mode, ttl_drops_total }
                                    * onCommand(...): Ping/Ack and admin (ForgetAllPairings, LoadReplay/StartReplay/ExitReplay). NACK via Ack{ok: false, code, msg} for invalids.
                                    * Watchdog hook: pet on progress; do not pet on hang; graceful SIGTERM (FR91–96).
                                    * Metric updates hook: Update counters, gauges, and timers respectively as previously described.
4.10.6 Concurrency model
                                    * Reactor thread (core): Owns ITransport, timers, reconnect budget, and command handling. Submits frames to BLE immediately. Applies resend pacing after reconnect.
                                    * Worker A: 20 Hz Alerts: calls onTick(): read → TTL → coalesce → CBOR → fragment → send → cache
                                    * Worker B: 1 Hz Status: builds status and calls IStatusSink.publish(...)


Ownership rules (must be followed):
                                    * Only the Reactor touches ITransport.
                                    * Workers allocate immutable buffers, then hand off to Reactor
                                    * No shared mutable state across threads; no back-pressure to SPSC queues.
                                    * MTU changes update slice_cap next tick only

4.11 Artificial Intelligence
4.11.1 System Overview and Objectives
The Embedded AI and Computer Vision subsystem forms the intelligence core of the ADAS prototype, enabling the ability to acquire and apply knowledge/information. Its main objective is to identify dangerous scenarios and alert the user accordingly. This is done by processing data from multiple sensors, including cameras, Radar, GPS, and IMU. The identification of dangerous scenarios is done by an accurate perception of the vehicle’s surroundings to identify potential hazards. 
More specifically, the sensor integration layer synchronizes raw data streams while the AI and Computer Vision layer focuses more on interpreting the data received from the sensors to extract environmental features for danger detection. The system’s sensors are fused to provide synchronized situational awareness under varying conditions from visual, spatial data. Specifically, lane detection, object detection, and tracking, as well as collision risk indication, are what this subsystem is extracting from the sensory data, which is then communicated to the mobile application subsystem to alert the user accordingly. This process will be integrated in a manner that allows for the easy addition of other sensors to the system; this will be reflected in the design of this subsystem. Sensor fusion of camera and radar has been shown to improve detection and tracking accuracy under adverse conditions using EKF-based systems [31].
Lane detection will be implemented using a forward-facing RGB camera as its primary input, continuously capturing at 30 frames per second. A convolutional neural network (CNN) trained from annotated road imagery will perform lane segmentation by identifying lane markings and drivable areas. The system continuously estimates lane curvatures and offsets in the vehicle to accurately trigger lane-departure warnings when appropriate under varying conditions. Many recent works use CNNs for robust lane detection and segmentation under challenging environmental conditions [32].
Object detection is the ability of the system to identify vehicles, pedestrians, and other obstacles. The model is trained on automotive datasets such as KITTI to ensure a high enough accuracy for traffic-related objects. Boxes digitally surround the identified objects to visualize the results of the subsystem. At times, certain conditions may affect the monocular camera to provide inaccurate results, which is particularly relevant in depth estimation for collision risk assessment. Fusing a monocular camera and radar (or LiDAR) is a common method to improve depth estimation and reduce errors [33], [34]. A Radar sensor is used in this case to provide accurate 3D positional data, while the camera provides visual context. These inputs will be fused to work hand-in-hand for the system to project the 3D depth points onto the 2D image pixels. This provides more accurate depth data to be used for collision prediction.
4.11.2 Method of Implementation
This subsystem’s goal is to provide an accurate and robust perception of the surroundings in real-time. More specifically, lane tracking, object detection, and depth estimation, along with collision risk assessment, are the major topics for this subsystem. The approach taken to tackle these topics involves integrating computer vision algorithms, sensor fusion techniques, and embedded AI optimization on the NVIDIA Jetson Nano. Real-world implementations of EKF / improved Kalman filter-based fusion between sensors have been demonstrated in works that target embedded or real-time systems [31]
4.11.2.1 Lane Detection
To design an accurate and robust lane detection algorithm, clean data collection and processing are necessary. This is done by initially computing the camera calibration matrix and distortion coefficients using a set of chessboard images. These are used to apply distortion correction to raw images. The undistorted images are processed through color transforms and gradients to create a thresholded binary image, and then a perspective transform to rectify the binary images to a bird's-eye view is applied. Lane pixels are then detected and fit to find lane boundaries to determine the curvature of the lane and vehicle position with respect to the center. The detected lane boundaries are then warped back onto the original image, and the output visually displays lane boundaries showing numerical estimation of lane curvature and vehicle position. Deep learning-based lane detection with spatial CNN / dual constraints has specifically been shown to improve accuracy under occlusion and poor lighting [32].
4.11.2.2 Object Detection
Similar to lane detection, object detection needs accurate data collection and a well-trained object detection model to perform dataset augmentation to then increase resilience to occlusions, varying lighting, and extreme weather conditions. Sensor fusion is also used to fuse 2D object detection with Radar depth data to generate and detect more accurate 3D object positions and distances. Not only do the Radar measurements give depth information, but it is also used to improve velocity estimation and detection reliability in low-visibility conditions. A multi-sensor Kalman filter is paired with the subsystem for the fusion of sensors to track object motion, handle temporary occlusions, and reduce false positives. In fact, experimental results in works combining a monocular camera and radar in EKF or improved filtering show significantly reduced depth estimation error [33].
4.11.2.3 Sensor fusion
Sensor fusion is extremely useful for depth estimation and collision risk assessment. The sensor data is again used to be fused, creating a more accurate and reliable depth map. Radar is the primary sensor fused with the monocular camera to refine distance estimates, especially for fast-moving objects, as well as combined with a Kalman filter for accounting for sensor noise and latency.  Other sensors, such as Lidar, can easily be integrated to be included in the sensor fusion process, giving the system additional information, enhancing the overall functionality and reliability. Using this data, relative velocities and distances between the ego frame of the vehicle and the detected objects are calculated. More specifically, the Time-to-Collision for each tracked object is calculated, defining safety thresholds to trigger forward collision warnings (set at 2 seconds), and integrating uncertainty estimates to reduce false positives and prevent unnecessary alerts to the driver.
This methodology ensures that this Embedded AI and Computer Vision subsystem will accurately detect lanes, track important objects, and provide real-time safety alerts.
4.12 Mobile App
This section will outline the mobile application design research for receiving and visualizing Bluetooth-enabled sensor data. The goal of this component is to deliver real-time sensory Bluetooth output data in a graphical user Interface (GUI). The research focuses on identifying suitable Bluetooth communication methods, programming languages for application logic, and strategies for mapping sensor data to a responsive GUI. The findings from this research will determine the design decision for the mobile application architecture to ensure seamless integration with the embedded programs.
4.12.1 Programming Languages and Environment
In this section, the candidate programming languages for developing the mobile application will be evaluated. The selected language must support reliable Bluetooth communication, handle real-time data, and be integratable with a GUI framework capable of portraying the surrounding environment and objects. Further criteria used to assess potential programming languages are based on other general requirements, such as platform capability, performance, integration with supported GUI frameworks, and possibly database frameworks, and ease of development. 
4.12.1.1 Programming Language Selection
The options evaluated for implementation were:
Option 1:  Dart 
Dart is a programming language modeled to be a modern interpretation of the programming language C [35]. The utilization of Dart in this instance is to support the Flutter framework. Flutter is a single code base service that delivers full cross-platform support for Android operating system devices, iOS devices, and even the web [36]. Dart, used with the Flutter framework, supports full cross-platform development for both Android and IOS and aligns well with live data visualization with its reactive model. It is also able to utilize Bluetooth functionality through third-party plug-ins. The disadvantage in using Dart with Flutter is, it is a generally unfamiliar language, and finding support for potential issues and bugs that arise may prove difficult.
Option 2: Java
Java is an object-oriented programming language where programs are built from classes [37]. Java was considered for its large community, helpful resources, and massive application programming interface (API) library, which contains many importable classes that can help with feature building on the app. Some of these APIs are Bluetooth-related and allow Bluetooth functionality in the application. The disadvantage of using Java was that, as a language, it is verbose, and the supported GUI frameworks were somewhat outdated. 
Option 3: Kotlin
Kotlin is a modern programming language created by JetBrains professionals that fixes Java’s verbosity based on best usage guidelines and developer experience. [38] Kotlin was officially supported by Google in 2017 and has an active community [38]. It is interoperable with Java and was designed to work side by side with Java, having access to all of Java’s libraries and frameworks [34]. As a result, Kotlin can utilize the same Bluetooth libraries offered to Java. The disadvantage of using Kotlin is that the support is smaller than that of Java, and it has far less cross-platform support for IOS.
For the mobile application of the ADAS, the final verdict is to use Kotlin with snippets of Java incorporated with the system to achieve the desired application, as this structure has the most benefits with the least drawbacks.
4.12.1.2 Integrated Development Environment
Android Studio is the official integrated development environment (IDE) for building Android applications with Android-specific tools and workflows [39]. Due to the selection of Kotlin as the programming language, Android Studio was also selected as the IDE, as the application will be primarily Android native. Android Studio provides compatibility with Android’s Software development kits (SDK) and has an integrated build and workflow system [39]. The instant user interface (UI) rendering feedback ensures we can iterate dynamically and seamlessly on user interface designs, which is important for UI upscale when adding more features to the application [39].
4.12.1.3 Build System 
The build system used will be Gradle, as it is an open-source build automation system that uses a domain-specific language (DSL), which in our case is Kotlin,  to define how projects are built, tested, and packaged [40]. With the selection of our IDE being Android Studio, Gradle works in tandem and is built in as the standard build tool for Android applications. It offers Android-specific build types, product flavours, variant management, dependency resolution, and signing configuration [40]. Gradle provides automated continuous integration environments for Gradle scripts, which are essential for incremental development where mobile features will be tested, packaged, and deployed one at a time [40].
4.12.2 Mobile Bluetooth Framework
In the selection process for the Bluetooth communication framework, several options were considered that were dependent on the programming language and format of data being sent from the onboard device. The sensor data can be packaged and sent via Bluetooth through a stream of bytes, JavaScript Object Notation (JSON), Concise Binary Object Representation(CBOR), or a comma-separated value CSV) file, which all affect which Bluetooth framework would be used. Since the app is being developed with Kotlin, potential frameworks include Android’s native Bluetooth Application Programming Interface (API ), RxAndroidBLE, or Kable. The sensor data will be transferred as compressed JSON in CBOR format through Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) channels due to how easily it can be parsed into JSON objects representing packets of alerts every tick. As a result, the team decided to use Android’s native Bluetooth API. The framework is capable of handling data deserialization and parsing in Kotlin and integrates seamlessly with Android Studio. 
4.12.3 Mobile Architecture
This section outlines the software architecture used to compose the application’s structure. The tasks handled by the application layer of the system include reception and management of real-time packaged CBOR Bluetooth data from the Jetson nano, alert tiering, organizing priority, relaying alerts and updates to the GUI, and managing potential input and configuration from the user. 
4.12.3.1 Software Architecture Patterns
In selecting the appropriate software design architectural pattern for the application, two prominent architectural patterns were considered: Model-View-Controller (MVC) and Model-View-ViewModel (MVVM). MVC was first considered as it separates the application into three components: model, view, and controller. The model manages the data and application logic, the view handles the user interface (UI) presentation, and the controller handles user input and updates the view and model [41].

However, in this context, due to tight coupling between the view and controller, and given that the system operates in real-time with lifecycle handling, we opted for the MVVM design.

The model represents the received Jetson Nano data management and handling, and passes the data to the view model. The ViewModel maintains the application state and updates the ui with data passed from the model [42]. The view renders the UI based on the Viewmodel state and receives user interaction to then be handled by the viewmodel [42].

4.12.3.3 Mobile User Interface Design
The user interface (UI) of the mobile application is designed to be concise and practical for the user to minimize distractions and maximize usefulness and safety. The goal is to present real-time data updates and object identification and relay that information to the user through area mapping, icons, and simple system alerts in a clear, non-distracting, and aesthetically cohesive manner. The interface is tailored for in-vehicle use, prioritizing driver focus and rapid comprehension.

The middle of the screen will be where the user will see their vehicle and the surrounding environment with real-time updates on objects in their proximity. This could possibly include object visualization, vehicle outlining, object tracking, lane departure detection, and potential collision zones. The bottom of the display will show dynamic pop-up warning or alert messages with relevant alert descriptions, colour-coded alert hierarchy, vehicle speed, and allow for user interaction with the system, including a settings button, Bluetooth reset button, and a user personalization button.
4.13 DevOps
DevOps is a set of practices that combines software development (Dev) and IT operations (Ops) to shorten the development lifecycle while ensuring high software quality and system reliability. In this project, DevOps principles will be applied to automate testing, streamline integration, and facilitate consistent deployment across all development environments.
The ADAS Demonstrator project benefits greatly from a DevOps-oriented approach because it integrates multiple hardware and software subsystems, including embedded components, artificial intelligence models, and mobile applications, that must work together seamlessly. The following tools and practices are implemented to support this workflow.
4.13.1 GitHub
GitHub serves as the central platform for version control, collaborative development, and integration throughout the project. All six team members contribute code concurrently using feature branches to reduce code conflicts and ensure controlled merging into the main branch. This approach maintains the stability of the main branch while allowing the team to track progress and review updates effectively over time.
Sensor drivers and integration code are managed using branches that allow team members to inspect and test logic before merging. Artificial intelligence models, including training scripts, model architectures, and preprocessing pipelines, are also stored on GitHub, where tags and releases can be used to manage model versions as they evolve. The mobile application code is similarly hosted in the same repository for consistency.
To enhance collaboration, GitHub Actions is used to automate testing pipelines, ensuring that code from each branch passes basic checks before merging. While automation may be a lower-priority feature early in the project, it provides a valuable foundation for improving reliability in later development stages. Documentation, such as README files and architecture markdown documents also hosted within the repository to help new members and future teams easily set up and understand the project structure.
To support this workflow, the project adopts a monorepo structure, illustrated in Figure 12, which organizes all major subsystems, including Artificial Intelligence, Embedded, Mobile, and DevOps, within a single repository. This structure clearly separates components while maintaining tight integration, making it easier to coordinate development across subsystems and support onboarding for future team members.
  

Fig 12: Mono-repository for ADAS
Although the project uses a single monorepo, each subsystem remains independently deployable. The embedded component, which includes sensor integration and fusion logic, can be built and deployed directly to the Jetson Nano through platform-specific deployment scripts contained in the embedded directory. The artificial intelligence models, stored within the AI directory, can be exported and loaded onto the Jetson Nano in a Docker container for inference during runtime. The mobile application, located in its own folder within the same repository, is deployed separately to Android or iOS devices through standard build tools such as Gradle. This structure ensures that while all components share a unified version control system, they can be developed, built, and deployed independently based on their specific runtime environments.
In addition to repository organization, the team employs branching and automated continuous integration and deployment workflows to maintain code quality and streamline deployment. As shown in Figure 13, when developers push code to the remote repository, GitHub Actions automatically runs testing and builds pipelines. Pull requests are reviewed and integrated only after passing these checks. Successful builds are packaged into Docker containers, which are then deployed to the Jetson Nano for testing and demonstration. This process ensures that all subsystems remain synchronized and that the deployed environment is consistent across team members [43].
  

Fig 13. GitHub workflow from development to Jetson Nano deployment
4.13.2 Docker
Docker provides a containerization platform that enables consistent development and deployment environments across team members. Each container includes the necessary runtime, libraries, and system dependencies, allowing the same build to execute seamlessly on local machines and the Jetson Nano.
This approach eliminates discrepancies between development environments, which can occur when different team members run slightly different configurations. By packaging the C++ perception software and artificial intelligence modules inside Docker containers, the team can ensure reproducibility and portability during testing and demonstration. Docker also allows developers to simulate the Jetson Nano environment locally before deployment. This helps validate performance, dependency management, and communication between subsystems without requiring constant access to hardware resources [44]. 
4.13.3 Databases
The project uses a combination of JSON and SQLite for data management on the Jetson Nano. JSON is used for lightweight data exchange between the embedded subsystem and the mobile application. It provides a structured and human-readable format for transmitting sensor values, alerts, and configuration data in real time. Since JSON is language-independent and easy to parse in both C++ and Java, it is ideal for ensuring consistent communication between the Jetson Nano and Android devices. 
For local storage, SQLite is used as the primary data management solution. SQLite is a lightweight file-based relational database that requires no server installation and provides efficient SQL querying. It supports structured storage for sensor logs, diagnostic data, and test results while maintaining minimal computational overhead. SQLite is deployed directly on the Jetson Nano to handle temporary data logging and debugging tasks. During active operation, data is streamed through JSON messages for real-time use by the mobile application, while SQLite logs provide historical data for analysis and validation. This dual approach ensures both efficient communication and reliable data persistence without introducing unnecessary system complexity [45].
